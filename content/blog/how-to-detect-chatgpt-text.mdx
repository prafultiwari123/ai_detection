ChatGPT has become the default writing assistant for millions of people, which means AI-generated text now shows up everywhere — student essays, marketing copy, product reviews, even personal emails. If you're trying to figure out whether a piece of text was written by ChatGPT, there are both manual signs to look for and automated tools that can help.

## Common Patterns in ChatGPT Writing

ChatGPT has a recognizable "voice" once you know what to look for:

**Overly balanced structure.** ChatGPT loves to present "on one hand, on the other hand" arguments, even when a topic doesn't call for even-handedness. Human writers are more likely to commit to a position.

**Repetitive sentence openers.** Phrases like "It's important to note," "In today's fast-paced world," and "When it comes to X" show up constantly in AI-generated text because they're statistically common transitional phrases the model has learned to lean on.

**Perfect grammar with flat rhythm.** Human writing naturally varies sentence length — short punchy sentences mixed with longer, winding ones. ChatGPT tends toward consistent, medium-length sentences with few grammatical errors, which paradoxically makes it feel less human.

**Excessive hedging.** Words like "generally," "often," and "can vary" appear frequently because the model is trained to avoid overly confident claims.

**List-heavy formatting.** Even in casual writing, ChatGPT gravitates toward bullet points and numbered lists to organize information — a habit less common in natural human prose.

## Why Manual Detection Isn't Reliable Enough

The problem with relying purely on these patterns is that skilled writers can mimic them naturally, and AI models are increasingly good at varying their output when prompted to "write more casually" or "add some imperfections." This is why relying on gut feeling alone leads to false positives and false negatives.

## How Line-by-Line Detection Works

Tools that analyze text line-by-line typically look at statistical properties of the writing — word predictability, sentence structure variance, and token-level patterns that differ between human and machine-generated text. Instead of giving a single "AI or not" verdict for an entire document, breaking the analysis down by line or paragraph helps identify exactly which sections are most likely AI-written, which is especially useful when a document is a mix of human edits and AI-generated drafts.

## What This Means in Practice

If you're an educator, editor, or content manager, no single method is foolproof. The most reliable approach combines:

1. Reading for the stylistic patterns above
2. Running the text through a detection tool for a second opinion
3. Considering context — did the writer have time to produce this? Does it match their usual style?

AI detection is probabilistic, not absolute. Treat results as one input into your judgment, not a final verdict.
