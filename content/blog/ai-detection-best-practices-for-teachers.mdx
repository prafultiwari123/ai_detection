AI detection tools have become common in classrooms, but using them well requires more nuance than just checking a percentage score. Here's a practical framework for educators.

## Treat Detection Scores as a Starting Point, Not a Verdict

No AI detector is 100% accurate, and false positives are a documented, real problem — particularly for non-native English speakers and students who write in a formal, structured style. A high AI-probability score should prompt a conversation with the student, not an automatic accusation or penalty.

## Build in Process-Based Evidence

Rather than relying solely on the final submitted document, consider requiring:

- **Draft history** via Google Docs version history or similar tools, which shows the actual writing process over time
- **In-class writing samples** early in the term to establish a baseline of each student's natural writing style
- **Brief oral check-ins** where students explain their reasoning or argument — genuinely difficult to fake convincingly if they didn't write the piece themselves

## Set Clear, Explicit AI Policies

Ambiguity creates problems for everyone. Be explicit about what's allowed: Is AI okay for brainstorming but not drafting? For grammar-checking but not content generation? Different assignments might reasonably have different rules — a first draft might allow AI assistance while a final exam essay doesn't.

## Talk to Students Before Assuming Bad Faith

If a paper is flagged, the first step should be a conversation, not a disciplinary referral. Many false positives happen to students who are surprised and can easily explain their process. Approaching the conversation with curiosity rather than accusation leads to better outcomes and fewer unfair penalties.

## Consider Redesigning Assignments

Some educators have found it more effective to redesign assignments to be less easily automatable in the first place — incorporating personal reflection, current events, in-class components, or highly specific prompts tied to class discussion. This reduces reliance on detection tools altogether.

## Stay Updated

AI detection is a fast-moving field, and both the AI models and the detection tools built to catch them are constantly evolving. A policy or tool that worked well a year ago may need revisiting as new models produce more human-like text.

## The Balance to Strike

The goal isn't to catch every instance of AI use — it's to maintain fair academic standards while giving students genuine room to explain themselves. Detection tools are useful evidence, but they work best as one part of a broader, more humane process.
